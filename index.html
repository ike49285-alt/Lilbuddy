    Time runs at the rate set in the bar below, from a day to a hundred thousand years per second. At a year per second or slower the seasons show: snow piles up on the peaks in winter and the river swells with the spring melt. The rains begin light on a young mountain front still being pushed upward, so the first water is a trickle. Over millions of years it finds its way, cuts gorges, captures neighbouring streams and builds a delta, while ice ages pull the sea back across the shelf and let it return.

      Life begins in the sea: plankton, seaweed and microbial mats. Freshwater algae arrive once the river reaches the coast. Species split when the river, ice or the coast cut a population in two, and new forms appear by mutation. Plants need more complexity before they can live out of water; once they do, they green the land and hold the soil, so vegetated slopes erode more slowly. Animals start as simple fish that graze the algae and plankton. Limbs and lungs can evolve, and fish that have them do a little better in warm, weedy shallows; with real legs and lungs, and land plants to eat, they can walk out of the water. Nothing makes it happen on a schedule. Tap a species to see where it lives.
